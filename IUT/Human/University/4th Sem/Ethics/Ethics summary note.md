## Lecture 01: Introduction & The Nature of Ethics

### 1. Conceptual Foundations
- **Definition of Ethics:** The philosophical study of morality. While morality is the set of beliefs and behaviors regarding right and wrong, ethics is the active process of reflection, justification, and systematization of those beliefs.
- **The Descriptive vs. Normative:** 
    - **Descriptive Ethics:** Describes what people *do* believe (Sociology/History).
    - **Normative Ethics:** Prescribes what people *should* believe and do (Philosophy).
- **Meta-Ethics:** Investigates the origin and meaning of ethical concepts (e.g., "What does 'good' mean?").

### 2. Engineering as Social Experimentation
Engineering projects are not just technical tasks; they are experiments conducted on the public.
- **Experimental Control:** Unlike lab experiments, engineering projects have limited control over environment and subjects.
- **Monitoring:** Ethical engineering requires continuous monitoring of the system after deployment to identify unforeseen risks.
- **Informed Consent:** The moral requirement that the public be aware of risks. In engineering, this is often handled through public hearings, environmental impact statements, and safety standards.

### 3. Case Studies & Pathological Decision-Making
- **Ford Pinto (1970s):** The company used a **Cost-Benefit Analysis** (CBA) that valued a human life at $200,000. They concluded that paying for lawsuits was cheaper than retrofitting a safety shield. This case is the classic example of utilitarian logic being used to justify a violation of human rights.
- **Challenger Disaster (1986):** Highlighted the **"Normalization of Deviance."** This is the process where technical anomalies (O-ring erosion) that don't immediately lead to failure are re-categorized as "acceptable risks" rather than "warning signs."
- **The Tension:** Engineers (Technical Judgment) vs. Managers (Organizational/Financial Judgment). The "Take off your engineering hat and put on your management hat" quote from the Challenger launch decision is a core warning in professional ethics.

## Lecture 02: Professionalism and the Social Contract

### 1. The Social Contract of Engineering
A profession is defined by a **Social Contract** with society:
- **Autonomy:** Society grants the profession the right to self-regulate (licensing, standards).
- **Prestige & Pay:** Society provides high social status and financial rewards.
- **Responsibility (The Return):** In exchange, the profession guarantees that its members will hold the **safety, health, and welfare of the public** paramount.

### 2. The Standard of Care
The **Standard of Care** is the level of performance that is common to, or expected of, a professional in the same field and situation.
- **Legal vs. Ethical:** Following the law is the "minimum" standard. Ethical behavior often requires going beyond the law (e.g., reporting a risk that is not yet regulated).
- **Negligence:** A failure to meet the standard of care.

### 3. Whistleblowing: The DeGeorge Framework
Whistleblowing is the act of an employee alerting the public or a regulatory agency to a serious problem within their organization.
- **When is it Permissible?** (Criteria 1-3)
    1.  The product/policy will do serious harm.
    2.  The engineer has reported the threat to their supervisor.
    3.  Internal channels have been exhausted.
- **When is it Obligatory?** (Criteria 4-5)
    4.  The engineer has documentary evidence.
    5.  There is a high probability that going public will prevent the harm.

## Lecture 03: Normative Ethical Theories

### 1. Utilitarianism (Focus on Outcomes)
*Core Idea:* The right action is the one that produces the "greatest good for the greatest number."
- **Jeremy Bentham's Hedonistic Calculus:** A quantitative approach measuring Intensity, Duration, Certainty, Propinquity (nearness), Fecundity (multiplication), Purity, and Extent.
- **John Stuart Mill:** Introduced qualitative differences (higher vs. lower pleasures).
- **Act vs. Rule:** Act Utilitarianism looks at the specific consequence of one deed; Rule Utilitarianism looks at the consequences of everyone following a specific rule.

### 2. Duty Ethics / Deontology (Focus on Intent & Rules)
*Core Idea:* Actions are inherently right or wrong, regardless of consequences.
- **Immanuel Kant's Categorical Imperative:**
    - **Formula of Universality:** Act only on a maxim you would want to become a universal law.
    - **Formula of Humanity:** Never treat people as a *mere* means to an end, but always as an end in themselves.
- **W.D. Ross's Prima Facie Duties:** Duties that are binding "at first sight" (e.g., Non-maleficence, Beneficence, Fidelity, Gratitude, Justice). When they conflict, one must use intuition/reason to determine the **Actual Duty**.

### 3. Virtue Ethics (Focus on Character)
*Core Idea:* Ethics is about *being* a certain kind of person rather than following rules.
- **Aristotle's Eudaimonia:** Human flourishing achieved through **Virtue (Arete)**.
- **The Golden Mean:** Every virtue is a balance between two vices (e.g., Courage is the mean between Cowardice and Recklessness).
- **Phronesis:** Practical wisdom developed through experience, allowing an engineer to navigate "gray" situations.

### 4. Global Perspectives
- **Indian Ethics:** Focus on **Dharma** (cosmic order and individual duty). It emphasizes that an engineer's work must contribute to the balance of society and nature.
- **Muslim Ethics:** Based on **Amanah** (trust). The Earth is a trust from God, and the engineer is a **Khalifah** (steward) responsible for sustainable development and the protection of life.

## Lecture 04: Problem-Solving & Decision Models

### 1. The Potter Box Walkthrough
A tool for dissecting complex dilemmas:
1.  **Definition:** Collect all factual data (the "Who, What, Where, When").
2.  **Values:** Identify competing values (Safety, Profit, Privacy, Loyalty, Career).
3.  **Principles:** Apply ethical frameworks (e.g., "What would Kant say?" vs. "What would a Utilitarian say?").
4.  **Loyalties:** Rank your responsibilities (Public > Profession > Client > Employer > Self).

### 2. Line Drawing: The Boundary Analysis
Used when a case falls between a "Clearly Right" (Positive Paradigm) and "Clearly Wrong" (Negative Paradigm).
- **Method:**
    1.  Identify the features of the paradigms (e.g., Amount of gift, Intent of giver, Timing).
    2.  Assign a weight to each feature.
    3.  Compare the current case's features to the paradigms.
- **Insight:** If the current case shares more features with the Negative Paradigm, it should be treated as such.

### 3. Flowcharting: Path Analysis
Used to visualize the consequences of a series of decisions.
- **Key Question:** "What happens if we choose X?" -> "Does it lead to a safe outcome or a violation of our code?"
- **Benefit:** Helps identify "Dead Ends" where no ethical path remains.

### 4. The Creative Middle Way
The goal is to resolve a conflict without choosing one value at the expense of another.
- **Logic:** "Can we satisfy both Value A and Value B through a third option?"
- **Example:** A company needs to dispose of chemicals (Value: Profit/Survival) but cannot pollute the river (Value: Safety).
    - *Choice 1:* Pollute (Violates Safety).
    - *Choice 2:* Close plant (Violates Profit).
    - *Creative Middle Way:* Invest in a new recycling process that turns waste into a byproduct, potentially creating a new revenue stream.

## Scenario-Based Ethical Q&A

**Q. 1: The "Hospitality" Dilemma.** An engineer is overseeing a large construction project. The lead contractor, who has been performing well, invites the engineer to an expensive weekend golf resort to "discuss future collaboration." The engineer's company policy on gifts is vague. How should the engineer analyze this using **Line Drawing**?

**ANS:** The engineer must establish a Positive Paradigm (PP) and a Negative Paradigm (NP). The PP would be a small token, like a company-branded pen or a business lunch at a local cafe (clearly acceptable hospitality). The NP would be a large cash bribe delivered in secret before a contract bid (clearly unacceptable bribery). Features to analyze include: 
1. **Value:** The golf resort is high-value (moves toward NP). 
2. **Timing:** If no active bids are open, it is less suspicious than if a new contract is being negotiated (moves toward PP). 
3. **Intent:** Is the intent "friendship" or "influence"? 
4. **Public Perception:** Would a third party see this as a bribe? 
Since the resort stay is high-value and specific to the contractor, it shares more features with the Negative Paradigm. The engineer should decline the resort stay but offer to meet for a standard business lunch instead.

**Q. 2: The Safety-Cost Trade-off.** A software engineer discovers a bug in an automated braking system that has a 1 in 1,000,000 chance of failing in extreme cold. Fixing the bug will delay the product launch by six months, costing the company $10 million and potentially leading to layoffs. Using **Utilitarianism**, should the launch proceed?

**ANS:** A Utilitarian analysis (specifically CBA) would weigh the "Total Utility." 
- **Costs of Delay:** $10M loss, employee layoffs, and loss of investor trust. 
- **Costs of Launch:** The statistical probability of death or injury. 
If the cost of a potential life (often valued by regulators at ~$9-10M) multiplied by the probability (1/1,000,000) is significantly lower than the $10M loss, a strict *Act Utilitarian* might justify the launch. However, a *Rule Utilitarian* would argue that launching a known faulty safety system undermines public trust in the entire engineering profession, leading to a much greater long-term "negative utility." Therefore, the Rule Utilitarian would mandate the fix.

**Q. 3: Environmental Conflict.** A chemical plant is the primary employer in a small town. New regulations require a 50% reduction in a specific non-toxic but "smelly" emission. The current filtration technology is too expensive, and installing it would make the plant unprofitable, leading to its closure. How can a **Creative Middle Way** be applied here?

**ANS:** The conflict is between the town's **Economic Survival** (Profit/Jobs) and the town's **Quality of Life** (Clean Air/Lack of Nuisance). 
Instead of choosing between "Close the plant" or "Ignore the regulation," the engineer should look for a third path:
1. **Process Modification:** Can the chemicals be used at a lower temperature to reduce the smell without expensive filters?
2. **Community Compensation:** Can the company invest in a local park or community center to "offset" the nuisance while they phase in the technology over a longer, negotiated timeline?
3. **Byproduct Research:** Can the "smelly" emission be captured and sold as a raw material to another industry? 
The best Creative Middle Way would be seeking a "Synthetic" solution—like a low-cost additive that neutralizes the odor at the source without requiring the expensive filtration system.

**Q. 4: Professional Responsibility.** An engineer notices that a senior colleague is consistently bypassing a minor safety check to meet deadlines. The colleague is a mentor and a friend. According to the **DeGeorge Criteria**, is the engineer obligated to whistleblow?

**ANS:** The engineer is not yet obligated to whistleblow to the public. According to DeGeorge:
1. **Serious Harm:** Bypassing safety checks *could* lead to serious harm, but the engineer must first determine the severity.
2. **Internal Reporting:** The engineer *must* first speak to the colleague or their immediate supervisor. Whistleblowing is only permissible after internal channels are exhausted.
3. **Evidence:** The engineer should start documenting the instances where the check was bypassed.
Whistleblowing is a "last resort." The actual duty here is to address the issue internally to ensure the **Social Contract** of protecting the public is maintained without unnecessarily damaging the organization or the colleague's career.
